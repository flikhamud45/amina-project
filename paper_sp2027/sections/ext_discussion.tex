\subsection{Status of the Measurements}
\label{ext:disc-status}

Every measurement of the paper, except the attribution study of the integer OPT-125M
(Table~\ref{tab:quality-attr}), which no job of the package runs, comes from one run of the artifact's
run package on one platform, one \PlatformGpu{} and 8 threads of an \PlatformCpu{}, with one version of
the code; each
implementation improvement is measured against the code path it replaces on the same queries
(Appendix~\ref{app:eval-improve}). Models are measured in full, except where a table gives a build of
$b$ of $L$ blocks and the long prompts of Appendix~\ref{app:eval-long}, which extrapolate 2- and
3-block builds; the security parameters, the setup proof's size and the per-prefix comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:gen} follow from the byte model. Peak memory is not yet reported \todo{peak memory}.

\subsection{Real Weights, Model Quality and V1}
\label{ext:disc-real}

\para{Real weights and model quality.} The costs were measured with random int8 weights. They
depend only on the shapes, except for the compressed size of the claims and of V1's values. On the
certified OPT-1.3B, real claims compress better than random ones: v0's proof is \RealProofSmaller{}\% smaller
(\RealOptMKLongReal{} against \RealOptMKLongRandom{}\,GB in $\Kpre$ at 2{,}048 tokens), while V1's values take about the same
space, so V1 shrinks the proof by \VOneRealOptMLongC{}$\times$ in mode $\sC$ at 2{,}048 tokens against \VOneRandomOptMLongC{}$\times$
with random weights; some residual operations of real OPT weights also have multipliers above $2^{29}$
and stay clear under (P3). The prover's and the verifier's times are within \RealProveWithin{}\% and
\RealVerifyWithin{}\% of random weights' (Appendix~\ref{app:eval-real}). In $\Kpre$ at 2{,}048 tokens
\RealAcceptedClause{}, and every tamper rejected. For the full real Llama-2-7B, with the main recipe of
Section~\ref{sec:quality}, \RealLlamaAccepted{} of \RealLlamaQueries{} queries are accepted and \RealLlamaTampersRejected{} of \RealLlamaTampers{} tampers
rejected, with a \RealLlamaProof{}\,GB proof (\RealLlamaSmaller{}\% smaller than with random weights) checked in \RealLlamaVerify{}\,s on 8 threads of the
\PlatformCpu{} (\RndLlamaVerify{}\,s with random weights). At 64 tokens both modes accept every query and reject every
tamper, with proofs \RealLlamaShortSmaller{}\% smaller and the verifier within \RealLlamaShortVerifyWithin{}\% of random weights; V1 makes
its 2{,}048-token proof \VOneRealLlamaRatio{}$\times$ smaller (\VOneRatioLlama{}$\times$ with random weights). With smoothing through the norm's gains, the certified OPT-125M,
OPT-1.3B and OPT-6.7B are within factors of \QualityRatioOptS{}, \QualityRatioOptM{} and \QualityRatioOptL{} of fp32 at 2{,}048 tokens
(Section~\ref{sec:quality}); with one scalar gain OPT-6.7B is \QualityScalarExcessOptL{}\% above. With a fixed public prefix and a balance of
the SwiGLU product, OpenLLaMA-7B (Llama-2-7B's exact benchmark structure) is within $\times$\LlamaOpenRatio{},
Llama-2-7B itself within $\times$\LlamaSevenRatio{} (95\% interval [\LlamaSevenCI{}]) and TinyLlama within
$\times$\TinyLlamaRatio{} of fp32 at 2{,}048 tokens, and Llama-2-13B within $\times$\LlamaThirteenRatio{} of fp16
([\LlamaThirteenCI{}]); with a per-pair balance of the q/k norms' gains as
well, Qwen3-1.7B is within $\times$\QwenMRatio{} and Qwen3-4B at $\times$\QwenLRatio{}. These graphs are the ones whose
costs we report. A per-token exponent at the SwiGLU product would bring Qwen3-1.7B to $\times$\QwenMExpRatio{}
and Qwen3-4B to $\times$\QwenLExpRatio{}, but it changes two operations per block and leaves the down projection
outside V1's cut ($\VOneDownClaims{}$ claims of a 2{,}048-token Llama-2-7B query), so we count it only as a possible
improvement and none of our cost tables uses it. Qwen3-4B forms a second massive activation, at the
first token after the prefix, that no public prefix we tried takes, and one static int8 scale per
residual stream cannot hold it; a per-token residual scale would change the graph and V1. Soundness holds for any committed integer model; these
numbers concern only how well that model serves.

\para{V1.} V1 is an option, not a replacement. At 2{,}048 tokens it makes the proof
\VOneRatioRandom{}$\times$ smaller with random weights, but it makes the prover about \VOneProverRatioAbout{} times slower and
the CPU verifier \VOneVerifyRatioLlama{}$\times$ slower than v0's on the full Llama-2-7B at 2{,}048 tokens
(\VOneVerifyRatioLong{}$\times$ on the 2{,}048-token builds of Table~\ref{tab:eval-v1}). For the client alone (transfer plus
verification), V1 is nevertheless faster than v0 on links slower than about \BreakevenVOneFaster{}\,Gb/s, and it beats
re-execution down to \BreakevenLinkVOne{}\,Gb/s, against \BreakevenLinkLong{}\,Gb/s for v0. Llama-2-13B at 2{,}048 tokens is
measured as the build of Table~\ref{tab:eval-v1}. Mode $\sK$ and the streaming GPU verifier do not
support V1 yet, and V1 assumes one requantisation multiplier per operation, which excludes
per-row weight scales ($\times\RowScalesLo{}$--$\RowScalesHi{}$ on OPT-125M at 128 tokens).

\subsection{A Client's Options in Detail}
\label{ext:disc-options}

Table~\ref{tab:options} compares the options of a client that wants one Llama-2-7B answer checked.
We add the details behind each row.

\para{Re-execution.} A client that downloads the \WeightsLlama{} of int8 weights once can re-run every
query. The integer forward pass is bit-identical to the claims and so a complete check; it took
\CpuReexecShort{}\,s at 64 tokens and \CpuReexecLong{}\,s at 2{,}048 tokens on 8 threads of an \PlatformCpu{}, and \GpuIntShort{} and
\GpuIntLong{}\,s on an \PlatformGpu{} with int8 tensor cores (Table~\ref{tab:options}). Floating-point
re-execution (fp32 on the CPU: \CpuFloatShort{} and \CpuFloatLong{}\,s; fp16 on the
GPU: \GpuFloatShort{} and \GpuFloatLong{}\,s) cannot confirm an exact answer. A CPU client that could keep the weights can
instead keep a key derived from them (mode $\Kpre$) and run our verifier, which takes \CpuVerifyShort{} and \CpuVerifyLong{}\,s,
\CpuRatioShort{}$\times$ and \CpuRatio{}$\times$ faster than exact re-execution.
On a GPU, re-execution wins: our streaming GPU verifier takes \OptMGpuVerify{}\,s for OPT-1.3B in $\Kpre$ at 2{,}048
tokens, against \OptMGpuFloat{}\,s for fp16 re-execution on the same GPU (Section~\ref{sec:eval-reexec}).

\para{GPU confidential computing.} An H100-class GPU runs the whole model inside an attested
enclave. A measurement study finds little overhead inside the GPU and attributes most of the
penalty, below 7\% for most LLM queries and close to zero for larger models and longer sequences,
to encrypted CPU--GPU transfers over PCIe~\cite{gpucc}. The client checks an attestation rather
than a proof, and the weights stay hidden from it. The guarantee rests on the vendor's hardware,
its attestation service and the enclave's resistance to side channels.

\para{CommitLLM-style audit.} CommitLLM~\cite{commitllm2026} adds 12--14\% to serving. Its routine
audit opens one token of 1--5\% of the responses and 10 of 80 layers at that token, so an edit
passes with probability \SokCommitOpened{}\% when the token is opened and about \SokCommitRateAbout{}\% per one-token response at
a 5\% audit rate (Table~\ref{tab:sok}). Its client holds secret Freivalds vectors precomputed from
the weights, and attention is audited, not verified. For Llama-3.1-8B, whose 32 layers of width
4{,}096 match Llama-2-7B's, it reports 144\,kB of retained trace state per token and a client check
of 0.66\,ms per audited token for the routine 10 layers (1.98\,ms for all 32) on an Apple M4, plus
about 0.5\,MiB per audited token when the sampler's logits are opened; for Llama-70B the client's
key is about 48\,MB.

\para{zkSNARKs.} zkLLM~\cite{zkllm} proves a 2{,}048-token Llama-2-7B prompt in 10.3\,min on an
A100, with a 183\,kB proof that its verifier checks in 2.36\,s, and hides the weights. zkAgent,
which proves a generated sequence in one pass, reports 0.30\,s per token for GPT-2~\cite{zkagent}.
On a 2{,}048-token Llama-2-13B prompt our prover on the \PlatformGpu{} is \ZkllmProverFactor{}$\times$ faster than zkLLM, but zkLLM
also proves the operations without weights and hides the weights (Table~\ref{tab:eval-compare}).
On the \PlatformGpu{} itself, zkLLM's public demo (commit 993311e of its repository) takes about
\ZkllmSameGpuProverLlama{}\,s to prove a 2{,}048-token Llama-2-7B prompt and \ZkllmSameGpuProverLlamaL{}\,s for Llama-2-13B,
\ZkllmSameGpuFactorLlama{}$\times$ and \ZkllmSameGpuFactorLlamaL{}$\times$ our prover's time on the same prompts (mode $\sC$, interactive,
as in Table~\ref{tab:eval-compare}). These times count zkLLM's proof binaries alone, not its one-time
setup and commitment or the scripts around the binaries; they were measured on two decoder layers
(three repetitions each) and multiplied by the number of layers, the first skip connection of each
layer, which its demo does not run, being counted as the second. They cover the decoder layers only.

\para{Ours.} In mode $\sC$ the client holds the 32-byte Merkle roots of the weights and no weights. A 64-token
proof is \ProofLlamaShortC{}, about \ProofRatioLlamaShort{}$\times$ smaller than the int8 weights, and is checked in \VerifyLlamaShortCCpu{} on 8
threads of the \PlatformCpu{} or \VerifyLlamaShortCGpu{} on the \PlatformGpu{}. Of it, \ColumnsLlamaShort{} are opened columns, which do not shrink with
shorter prompts. At 2{,}048 tokens the proof is \ProofLlamaLongC{} (\ProofLlamaLongK{} in $\Kpre$), about the size of the
weights, and takes \TransferLlamaLong{}\,s to transfer at 10\,Gb/s. A client that sends
more than about \BreakevenQueriesShort{} short prompts therefore receives more bytes than a download of the
weights, which are only \BreakevenQueriesLong{}$\times$ the size of one long prompt's proof, and the protocol pays off only for a client that cannot store or run the model, or
will not (Figure~\ref{fig:breakeven}). A $\Kpre$ client needs the weights once, to compute its key
(\KpreKeyLlama{}\,s for Llama-2-7B on 8 threads of the \PlatformCpu{}), and then keeps \KpreStoreGB{}\,GB instead of them:
\KpreKeyMB{}\,MB of secret vectors and folded rows and the \KpreEmbedMB{}\,MB embedding table. A provider may also serve with the exact model and prove
later, on request, with the same proof; it then still pays the integer engine's cost over fp16
serving (\ServeOptMUOneOverUTwo{}$\times$ on OPT-1.3B), and only the queries it proves are checked. On the \PlatformGpu{}, proving a 2{,}048-token OPT-1.3B prompt costs
\ServeOptMOverUOneRange{}$\times$ our exact forward pass and \ServeOptMOverUTwoRange{}$\times$ an fp16 one\TodoServingExt{}. Sending one opening and one fold for a batch of
queries whose claims are all fixed before the challenges amortises the opened columns over the
batch: on OPT-1.3B at 64 tokens, 64 prompts in one mode-$\sC$ proof take \ServeBatchMBPerTokenC{}\,MB per token
against \ServeSingleMBPerTokenC{}\,MB for one prompt, and on the full Llama-2-7B \BatchLlamaSize{} prompts of 64 tokens take
\BatchLlamaPerQuery{}\,MB each against \BatchLlamaSingle{}\,MB for one, within \BatchLlamaWithin{}\% of the claims plus a shared
\BatchLlamaShared{}\,MB divided among the prompts at every batch size we measured.

\subsection{Limitations in Detail}
\label{ext:disc-limits}

\para{Proof size and the verifier.} Even with V1, the proof grows with the prompt, because it
carries one value for every output of every weight operation. For Llama-2-7B it reaches the size of
the int8 weights at about \BreakevenTokens{} tokens without V1 and, by the byte model, at about \BreakevenTokensVOne{} tokens
with it. Only committing the activations and proving the operations without weights, as zkLLM
does, would remove this growth; we leave it to future work. The verifier recomputes attention, so
its cost grows with the square of the prompt length; at 2{,}048 tokens the operations without
weights take about \CpuDeriveShareLong{}\% of the CPU verifier's time on the full Llama-2-7B even with the native
kernels. A CPU client that could keep the weights still saves time over exact re-execution by
keeping a $\Kpre$ key instead (\CpuRatioShort{}$\times$ at 64 tokens and \CpuRatio{}$\times$ at 2{,}048, Section~\ref{sec:eval-reexec}),
but on a GPU re-execution is faster than our verifier. The case for the protocol is
therefore a client that does not hold the weights or the hardware to run them, not a faster way to
compute an answer.

\para{Weights and trust.} The protocol is not zero-knowledge: the claims of about
$\lceil k/T\rceil$ queries reveal a weight matrix with rows of length $k$ (about six 2{,}048-token
queries for Llama-2-7B's down projection, $k=11{,}008$), so mode $\sC$ targets open-weight models.
For private weights it shares the limitation that Hollow-LLM points out for
zkSNARKs~\cite{hollowllm}: the commitment binds the provider to one fixed model, but a client that
sees neither the weights nor a range proof on them does not learn which model that is, and weights
with a degenerate structure keep the declared shapes while being cheap to evaluate. With an
untrusted commitment (Section~\ref{sec:setup-proof}), the bound (P4) of V1 and the claim range of
v0 must be published with the model; the setup proof of Llama-2-7B takes \SetupProverLlama{} to prove and
\SetupVerifierLlama{} to verify. The exact sampler (Section~\ref{sec:gen-sampling}) is
a fixed approximation of softmax sampling (total-variation distance with median \TvTopK{} on OPT-1.3B at
$\tau=1$, $k=50$), the proof
does not show that the client's seed was random, and nucleus sampling is not implemented.

\para{The attack and the theorem.} The lower bound needs condition (H), which we establish for
random ensembles with independent rows and on a constructed worst-case network, not for a trained
network, whose rows are correlated. On trained networks we report the detection of the best attacks
we found, which bounds each sampler's worst-case detection from above. We do not claim that the
worst-case instance embeds in a transformer, since the LayerNorm before the MLP prevents feeding it
the required patterns (Remark~\ref{rem:scope}). With held-out ranges, the adaptive game on the MNIST
MLP leaves every implementable value-aware sampler, and their best mixture, below $27.7/N$ per path;
only gradient saliency, which needs the whole model and so no spot-checking verifier can compute,
exceeds it. Our OPT-6.7B attack writes values outside the neurons' natural ranges; on OPT-1.3B, edits
that keep every value inside ranges calibrated on held-out text succeed (Section~\ref{sec:break}),
and so do they on OPT-6.7B (Table~\ref{tab:inrange}). We deliberately do not develop a method for
steering the responses of instruction-tuned assistants.
