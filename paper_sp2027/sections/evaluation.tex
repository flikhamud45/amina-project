\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}

We measure how often the path protocol accepts a single-site edit (Section~\ref{sec:eval-attack}),
what the defence costs and what each improvement changes (Section~\ref{sec:eval-cost}), V1 against
v0 (Section~\ref{sec:eval-v1}), when verifying is cheaper than re-running the model
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-reexec}), and how we compare with published systems
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-compare}). Generation and the quality of the integer models are evaluated in
Sections~\ref{sec:gen} and~\ref{sec:quality}.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:eval-setup}

\para{Implementation.} The protocol is written in Python and PyTorch, with Triton kernels for the
GPU claim decoder, the fused exact attention and V1's GKR prover, and a C++/OpenMP attention kernel
for CPU verifiers. Merkle trees use SHA-256 and the Fiat--Shamir transcript SHAKE-256; the field is
BabyBear and V1's logUp-GKR works over $\mathbb{F}_{p^8}$. We use $\lambda = 128$ unless stated
otherwise. We write v0 for the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:defence} and V1 for the variant of
Section~\ref{sec:v1}. The prover and verifier improvements change how each party computes, not a
byte that they exchange.

\para{Hardware.} The \emph{base implementation}, without the improvements, was measured with an
NVIDIA L40S (48\,GB) as prover and GPU verifier and 8 threads of an AMD EPYC 9334 as CPU verifier.
Each improvement, and V1, was measured on an NVIDIA RTX~2080~Ti (11\,GB) with 8 threads of an Intel
Xeon Silver 4114, against the previous code path (or v0) on the same queries; no improvement changed
a claim, a proof or a verdict. The full models with all improvements will be measured on the L40S
\todo{L40S: final run of every table in this section with the frozen code}. Times are medians after
a warm-up; proof sizes do not depend on the hardware.

\para{Models.} The image models are an MLP and LeNet-5 on MNIST, VGG-11, VGG-16 and ResNet-18 on
CIFAR-10, and a 224$\times$224 dog-versus-cat ResNet-18 that stands in for the unreleased classifier
of Anchuri et al.~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable}; int8 quantisation changed their accuracy by at most
0.4 points. The language models are GPT-2, OPT-125M/1.3B/6.7B, Llama-2-7B/13B and Qwen3-4B, with
random int8 weights for costs, which depend only on the shapes except for the size of the
compressed claims \todo{F3: real-weight end-to-end run of Qwen3-4B and OPT-6.7B}. A model too large
for the 2080~Ti is measured as $b$ of its $L$ blocks ($b/L$), the last one pruned. Real weights are
used for the attack on OPT-6.7B and for model quality (Section~\ref{sec:quality}).

% -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Attack and Its Detection}
\label{sec:eval-attack}

\para{Path protocol.} Section~\ref{sec:break} reports the attack in full; we summarise it here.
Probabilities of passing a path are computed exactly by dynamic programming over the paths. One
tampered neuron, followed by honest recomputation, passes a path with probability $1-1/N$
(99.6--99.8\% on the MNIST MLP), and 250 paths still accept it 61\% of the time. A triggered
backdoor passed 99.96\% of 2{,}300 end-to-end runs. On every CNN and test query one penultimate
neuron flips the prediction, including the binary ResNet-18 for which Anchuri et al.\ argue full
soundness, and on the real OPT-6.7B one FFN neuron changed the next token for all 40 prompts. Of
the samplers we tried, the best detects 2\% of the attacks built against it on the MLP
(Section~\ref{sec:break}). Catching one edited neuron with probability $1-2^{-40}$ takes
2{,}316--14{,}182 paths on the CNNs, which open the whole model and trace, and at least 305{,}193
paths for Llama-2-7B, about 13.7\,GB at 64 tokens, where our proof is 325\,MB at $\lambda = 128$.

\para{Our verifier against the same attacks.} The base implementation accepted all 3{,}792 honest
queries and rejected all 2{,}512 attacks, from one changed value or neuron to perturbed weights,
substitute models and forgeries that pass all checks but one: Freivalds' check rejected 1{,}601,
the column check 853, the Merkle check 56 and the range check 2. An adversarial review of V1 found
forgeries only on two crafted models that violate conditions the verifier now checks. Without the
setup proof (Section~\ref{sec:setup-proof}), a commitment that mixes two models passes about
$2^{-t}$ of the queries; with it, it is rejected at setup \todo{L40S: outcome counts with the final
code, including V1 and the setup proof}.

\para{Prover improvements.} Table~\ref{tab:eval-improve} measures each improvement alone. Opening
the committed columns took 62\% of Llama-2-7B's base prover at 64 tokens (1.105 of 1.78\,s), in
float64. Splitting each field element into four signed bytes makes the opening and the fold exact
int8 GEMMs with bit-identical results, and the prover 3.7--4.3$\times$ faster on Llama-2 blocks.
Int8 tensor cores also halve the forward pass's weight products on Llama-sized layers, and the
prover reuses the verifier's fused attention kernel. The claim encoder turned out to be bound by a
single-threaded copy of its output, so assembling the output on several threads gives
2.1--2.3$\times$; a fused Triton encoder with the same bytes is slower (1.74 against 1.54\,s on
OPT-1.3B), a negative result \todo{L40S: full Llama-2-7B and Llama-2-13B provers at 64 and
2{,}048 tokens}.

\para{Verifier improvements.} The base GPU verifier spent most of its time decoding the claims on
the host and passing about a dozen times over materialised $T\times T$ attention scores. Our GPU
decoder validates every offset on the host before launching a kernel and rejects exactly the inputs
that the host decoder rejects (tested on every truncation and on 400 random corruptions). Our fused
attention kernel computes the same integers without a $T\times T$ buffer; its fp32 sums are exact
because every partial sum is an integer below $2^{24}$. Together they make the GPU verifier
2.0--2.7$\times$ faster at 2{,}048 tokens. Afterwards, copies take 32\% of the CUDA time of an
OPT-1.3B query (12 blocks) and int8 GEMMs 4.4\%: data movement dominates, including the proof upload that V1
halves. On a CPU, a native exact attention kernel gains 1.5$\times$ at 2{,}048 tokens
\todo{L40S/EPYC: full-model verifiers with all improvements}.

\para{Memory and untrusted commitments.} \todo{F4: peak GPU and host memory per cell}. Without the
honest-commitment assumption (Section~\ref{sec:setup-proof}), Llama-2-7B needs a one-time setup
proof of 2.8\,GB (or 11.3\,GB), which raises its per-query column bytes from 124.7 to 168.5\,MB (or
134.9\,MB), less than 1\% of the proof at 2{,}048 tokens.

\para{Proof size.} V1 sends the int8 value (or shifted value) that the next operation reads instead
of each 32-bit result, and proves with a logUp-GKR that the hidden result lies in that value's
window (Section~\ref{sec:v1}). At 2{,}048 tokens its proof is 2.0--2.3$\times$ smaller than v0's on
every full or two-block build (Table~\ref{tab:eval-v1}); the full OPT-1.3B goes from 2.07 to
0.96\,GB. V1's own additions stay below 1\,MB per proof. The gain is smaller where parts that V1
leaves unchanged weigh more: 1.81$\times$ for the single Llama-2-13B block in $\sC$, whose opened
columns are a larger share, and 1.28--1.46$\times$ for GPT-2 at 64 tokens. With real weights a byte
model predicts 1.8--2.0$\times$ at 2{,}048 tokens, and the smoothed real OPT-125M measures
1.68$\times$ ($\sC$) and 1.77$\times$ ($\Kpre$) at 512 tokens \todo{real-weight V1 bytes at
2{,}048 tokens on the smoothed OPT graphs}.

\para{Cost.} The prover pays for the GKR, about one leaf per sent value (15.6\,ns per leaf for a
$2^{25}$-leaf instance on the 2080~Ti). At 2{,}048 tokens V1's prover thus takes about 10$\times$ as
long as v0's fast int8 prover (OPT-125M in $\sC$: 0.50 to 4.85\,s, 3.58\,s of it the GKR), still
seconds where the zkSNARK provers of Section~\ref{sec:eval-compare} take minutes. The CPU verifier
takes 1.6--1.9$\times$ v0's time at 2{,}048 tokens and 2.2--2.8$\times$ at 512, mostly for the two
exact products per value of its final check. V1 thus suits clients whose bandwidth matters more
than the prover's time \todo{L40S: V1 timings, including Llama-2-7B in $\sC$, which does not fit
the 11\,GB card with the GKR; streaming GPU verifier with V1}.

In mode $\sC$ the client holds only the commitment and cannot re-run the model; in $\sK$ and
$\Kpre$ it could. Table~\ref{tab:eval-reexec} times the integer forward pass, which is
bit-identical to the claims and so a complete check, and a floating-point decoder of the same
shapes, which is faster but cannot confirm an exact answer.

\para{CPU.} On 8 threads our $\Kpre$ verifier checks a 64-token Llama-2-7B query in about 1.5\,s,
against 3.5\,s for fp32 and 6.8\,s for integer re-execution. At 2{,}048 tokens it ties fp32
re-execution (85 against 86\,s) and is 2.3$\times$ faster than integer re-execution; recomputing the
operations without weights, mostly attention, takes 76\% of its time \todo{L40S/EPYC: full-model
CPU verifier with the native kernel against both re-executions on the same CPU}.

\para{GPU.} On a GPU, re-execution is faster. The fp16 decoder runs Llama-2-7B at 2{,}048 tokens in
0.57\,s on the 2080~Ti, while the base GPU verifier needed 4.95\,s in $\Kpre$ on the faster L40S.
Our verifier improvements extrapolate to about 1.1\,s for OPT-1.3B in $\Kpre$ on the 2080~Ti,
against 0.18\,s for its fp16 re-execution \todo{L40S: re-execution and the new GPU verifier on the
same GPU}.

\para{Where this leaves the verifier.} We therefore do not claim that verification beats
re-execution on a GPU. The GPU verifier suits an auditor or gateway that checks a provider without
holding the weights, and a CPU client that holds them saves 2.3--4.5$\times$ at short prompts and
beats exact re-execution at both measured lengths. Since the claims of about $\lceil k/T\rceil$
queries of $T$ tokens reveal a weight matrix with rows of length $k$, mode $\sC$ suits
open-weight models served by an untrusted provider. Re-running Llama-2-7B then takes its 6.7\,GB of
int8 weights and, to be fast, a GPU; verifying it takes a 32-byte root per matrix and a proof. At
2{,}048 tokens that proof (6.6\,GB, 5.3\,s at 10\,Gb/s) is about as large as the weights, and V1

Table~\ref{tab:eval-compare} compares our costs with published systems on matched models, whose
costs are as reported on their own hardware and checked against the sources. Our prover is faster
in every row with a measured value, by 46$\times$ (zkLLM, Llama-2-13B) to about 32{,}000$\times$ (ZKML,
VGG-16) against the zkSNARKs, partly because all of them but zkLLM ran on CPUs, and by 3.0$\times$
against Maverick. Our verifier is slower in two measured rows, against ZKML on VGG-16 (2.2$\times$)
and zkLLM on OPT-1.3B at 2{,}048 tokens (2.9$\times$), whose provers take minutes. Our proof is
larger in most rows: 3.6--180$\times$ against the other short-input proofs, 2{,}500--14{,}000$\times$
against zkLLM on OPT and 36{,}000--56{,}000$\times$ on Llama-2, where zkLLM's proof stays below
200\,kB. Where our proof is smaller, we are better on all three costs.

\para{What this paper's changes add.} The improvements leave the proof sizes unchanged and will
replace our timings \todo{L40S}. V1 halves the proof where it is largest, from 3.6$\times$ to
1.8$\times$ DeepProve's on GPT-2 with 512 tokens and from about 14{,}000$\times$ to about
6{,}500$\times$ zkLLM's on OPT-1.3B. Only committing the activations and proving the operations
without weights, as zkLLM does, would stop the proof from growing with the prompt. Our GPU
verifier's extrapolated 1.1\,s for OPT-1.3B in $\Kpre$ on the 2080~Ti approaches zkLLM's 0.90\,s
on an A100 \todo{L40S: zkLLM, and DeepProve if feasible, on our L40S}. zkAgent reports about
0.30\,s per token for GPT-2 generation~\cite{zkagent}; Section~\ref{sec:gen} compares generation
costs. CommitLLM and the other sampling-based schemes of Table~\ref{tab:sok} do not check every
operation of every query, so we do not compare their costs.
