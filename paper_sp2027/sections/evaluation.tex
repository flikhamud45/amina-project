\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:eval-setup}

\para{Implementation.} The protocol is written in Python and PyTorch, with Triton kernels for the GPU
verifier and C++/OpenMP kernels for the CPU verifier. Our \emph{streaming} GPU verifier uploads the
claims one weight operation at a time, overlapping the upload with its checks. Unless stated otherwise,
$\lambda=128$, every measurement uses Fiat--Shamir, and mode~$\sC$ uses codewords of at least $2^{\PlanLogPoints{}}$ points.

\para{Platform.} All our measurements come from one run of the artifact: one \PlatformGpu{} runs the
prover, the GPU verifier and GPU re-execution, and 8 threads of an \PlatformCpu{} the CPU verifier
and CPU re-execution. Other systems' costs are quoted from their papers; we also ran zkLLM's public
code on the \PlatformGpu{} \ZkllmSameGpuRun{}. Times are medians after a warm-up (\OptionsRuns{} runs
in Table~\ref{tab:options}, min--max spread $\le$\OptionsSpread{}\%).

\para{Models.} The image models are the six of Section~\ref{sec:break}. The language models are
GPT-2, OPT-125M/1.3B/6.7B, Llama-2-7B/13B and Qwen3-4B, built from their checkpoints with the
integer recipes of Section~\ref{sec:quality}; GPT-2, and prompts beyond a checkpoint's trained
context (4{,}096 tokens and more), use random int8 weights of the same shapes. Weight values matter
little: on the certified OPT-1.3B (both modes, 64 and 2{,}048 tokens) the prover's and the CPU
verifier's times are within \RealProveWithin{}\% and \RealVerifyWithin{}\% of those with random
weights, and v0's proof is \RealProofSmaller{}\% smaller with the real ones. The last block computes only the final
position, the one the logits need. Models are measured in full unless a table marks a build of $b$
of their $L$ blocks as $b/L$.

\para{Soundness tests.} Section~\ref{sec:break} reports the attack measurements. Our verifier
accepted all \FinalNHonest{} honest queries and rejected all \FinalNAttacks{} attacks (one changed
value or neuron, perturbed weights, substitute models, forgeries that pass all checks but one):
Freivalds' check rejected \FinalNFreivalds{}, the column check \FinalNColumnsCode{}, the Merkle
check \FinalNColumnsMerkle{} and the range check \FinalNRange{}\TodoCounts{}. In $\Kpre$ at 2{,}048 tokens,
\RealAcceptedClause{}, and every tampered proof of them is rejected.

\para{Costs.} In mode~$\sC$, the image models are proved in \CnnProveRange{}\,ms and verified in
\CnnVerifyRange{}\,ms, with proofs \CnnProofRatio{}$\times$ smaller than their int8 weights.
Llama-2-7B's 64-token proof is about \ProofRatioLlamaShort{}$\times$ smaller than its int8 weights
(Table~\ref{tab:eval-llm}). At 2{,}048 tokens the proof and the CPU verifier's time grow (OPT-6.7B:
\ProofOptLLong{}, \VerifyOptLLong{}): the claims, which grow with the prompt, make up
\ClaimsShareLlama{}\% of Llama-2-7B's \ProofLlamaLongCExact{} proof, and the verifier recomputes
attention over all position pairs. At this length the GPU verifier is \GpuVerifyFactor{}$\times$
faster than the CPU one, and a CPU client of Llama-2-7B in $\Kpre$ needs \ClientPeakLongKpre{}\,GB of memory. Proving a 2{,}048-token OPT-1.3B prompt on the \PlatformGpu{} costs
\ServeOptMKpreOverUOne{}$\times$ ($\Kpre$) and \ServeOptMCOverUOne{}$\times$ ($\sC$) our exact integer
forward pass, and \ServeOptMKpreOverUTwo{}$\times$ and \ServeOptMCOverUTwo{}$\times$ an fp16 one. Proving 64
of its 64-token prompts together in $\Kpre$ takes \ServeBatchKpre{}\,s, \ServeBatchOverUOne{} and
\ServeBatchOverUTwo{}$\times$ the two forward passes\TodoServing{}.

\para{Optimizations.} The prover makes the column opening and the fold exact int8 GEMMs by splitting
field elements into signed bytes (\ImproveOpenRange{}$\times$ faster on Llama-2 blocks), runs the weight
products on int8 tensor cores and attention in a fused exact kernel, frees activations once used and
encodes the claims on several threads. The GPU verifier decodes the claims on the GPU and reuses the
fused attention (\ImproveGpuvRange{}$\times$ at 2{,}048 tokens); the CPU verifier uses native kernels,
int32 claims, a tuned allocator and native field products for the $\Kpre$ key; V1 batches its final
checks and runs the GKR rounds in Triton. Where memory is short, the Llama-2 GPU verifier decodes the
claims one stream at a time and V1's Llama-2-7B runs keep the GKR's layers in bounded memory. None of
these optimizations changes a byte exchanged or a verdict; the extended version~\cite{fullversion}
measures each.

\para{CPU.} Without the weights, our $\Kpre$ verifier checks a 2{,}048-token query of the full
Llama-2-7B about as fast as exact re-execution with them (Table~\ref{tab:options}): \CpuVerifyLong{}\,s
on 8 threads of the \PlatformCpu{}, with native exact kernels including an AVX-512 integer attention. Exact re-execution on the same threads (the integer forward pass with
\CpuReexecName{}) takes \CpuReexecLong{}\,s and needs the \WeightsLlama{} of weights, where the
verifier holds a \KpreStoreGB{}\,GB key. At 64 tokens the two take \CpuVerifyShort{}\,s and
\CpuReexecShort{}\,s. Inexact inference of the same weights (PyTorch, llama.cpp~\cite{llamacpp}),
which cannot confirm the claims bit for bit, takes at least \CpuInexactLong{}\,s at 2{,}048 tokens
(\CpuInexactName{}).

\para{Break-even.} A client that receives a proof of $B$ bytes over a link of $R$ bit/s and
verifies it in time $t_v$ beats a re-execution taking $t_r$ when $8B/R+t_v<t_r$.
Below about \BreakevenQueriesShort{} queries of 64 tokens, mode-$\sC$ proofs cost fewer bytes than one
download of the \WeightsLlamaGB{}\,GB of int8 weights; at 2{,}048 tokens the weights are only
\BreakevenQueriesLong{}$\times$ one proof (\BreakevenQueriesVOne{}$\times$ with V1). A $\Kpre$ client
that could keep the weights verifies faster than exact re-execution on its CPU only on links faster
than \BreakevenLinkRange{}\,Gb/s\BreakevenVOneClause{} (Figure~\ref{fig:breakeven}).

\para{GPU.} On a GPU, re-execution is faster: on the \PlatformGpu{}, our streaming GPU verifier
checks a 2{,}048-token OPT-1.3B query in $\Kpre$ in \OptMGpuVerify{}\,s, against \OptMGpuFloat{}\,s
for fp16 re-execution on the same GPU\TodoGpuOneGpu{}. The GPU verifier suits an auditor or gateway
that checks a provider without holding the weights.

On the same \PlatformGpu{}, zkLLM's prover takes about \ZkllmSameGpuProverLlama{}\,s for a 2{,}048-token
Llama-2-7B prompt and \ZkllmSameGpuProverLlamaL{}\,s for Llama-2-13B, \ZkllmSameGpuFactorRange{}$\times$
ours, and \ZkllmSameGpuFactorShortRange{}$\times$ at \ZkllmSameGpuShortSeq{} tokens. Against published
numbers~\cite{zkcnn,zkml,zkgpt,deepprove,zktorch,maverick,zkllm} on matched models
(Table~\ref{tab:eval-compare}), our prover is faster in every row, by \CompareProverMin{}$\times$ to
about \CompareProverMax{}$\times$ against the zkSNARKs and \CompareMaverick{}$\times$ against Maverick;
our verifier is slower in \CompareVerifierSlowerRows{}; and our proof is larger in most rows, by up to
\CompareProofMax{}$\times$ against zkLLM, whose proof stays below 200\,kB. These factors mix hardware:
all the zkSNARKs but zkLLM ran on CPUs, and our prover on a GPU. The guarantees also differ: the
zkSNARKs also prove the weight-free operations, and many hide the weights, whereas our verifier
recomputes those operations and our protocol is not zero-knowledge. On OPT-1.3B, V1 narrows the
proof-size gap to zkLLM from about \CompareVOneBefore{}$\times$ to about \CompareVOneAfter{}$\times$.
Sampling-based schemes such as CommitLLM skip operations, so they appear only in
Tables~\ref{tab:sok} and~\ref{tab:options}.
