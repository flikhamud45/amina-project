\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Open-weight language models are often served by a provider the client does not control. The provider
may serve a cheaper model, or alter the computation on chosen queries to return answers of its
choice. In a \emph{proof of inference}, the provider sends a proof with each answer, and
the client's check fails with high probability unless the agreed model computed the answer on this
input. We target a client that has no GPU, does not store the weights, and holds only their 32-byte
Merkle roots (mode~$\sC$) or a \KpreStoreGB{}\,GB secret key derived once from them
(mode~$\Kpre$). The agreed model is an exact int8 network derived from the public
fp16 checkpoint by a deterministic, public quantisation recipe with fixed calibration data, so
anyone can recompute its commitment.

zkSNARK-based proofs~\cite{zkcnn,zkml,ezkl,zkllm,zkgpt,deepprove,zktorch} give this guarantee with
small proofs, but proving costs far more than inference: zkLLM needs about ten minutes on an A100
for one 2{,}048-token Llama-2-7B prompt~\cite{zkllm}. Trusted execution
environments~\cite{slalom,twinshield,veriattn,gpucc} are fast but trust the hardware vendor. Lighter
protocols check only a sample of the computation. In the path protocol of Anchuri et
al.~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable}, the provider commits to the execution trace with a Merkle tree,
and the client re-checks one random output-to-input path against a commitment to the weights. Other
designs sample chunks~\cite{slp}, layers~\cite{tensorcommitments,nanozk,commitllm2026} or
requests~\cite{spex,immaculate}. A different model leaves many inconsistent sites, so a sample is
likely to hit one. Anchuri et al.\ prove this \emph{other-model} soundness, note that one path
catches a single changed node with probability at most $1/N$ in a layer of width $N$, and leave
\emph{full} soundness, against any wrong answer, open for large models. We show that sampling
cannot give full soundness, and that checking every weight operation can, without a SNARK.

\para{The attack.} The provider needs no other model: it overwrites the value of one neuron and
recomputes every later layer honestly with the true weights. The trace is then inconsistent at one
site, which a random path visits with probability $1/N$ (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). On
OPT-6.7B, editing one feed-forward neuron, to a value outside its natural range, changes the next
token for \AttackOptLSucceeded{} of \AttackOptLPrompts{} prompts. Editing a few neurons within ranges
calibrated on held-out text does the same on OPT-1.3B and OPT-6.7B. Applied to triggered inputs only, the edit is a backdoor
that leaves the committed weights clean. On a dog-vs-cat ResNet-18, one changed neuron flips every
test query and passes a path with probability \PathPassResNet{}, refuting the full soundness that
Anchuri et al.\ state for binary classifiers. More paths do not help at a useful cost: detection
with probability $1-2^{-40}$ on Llama-2-7B takes about \PathsLlamaAbout{} paths per query.

\para{A lower bound for spot-checking.} The attack is not specific to the path protocol. After
honest recomputation, the forged trace is the honest trace of a model that differs from the
committed one in one weight row. Consider a verifier that makes $q$ queries per layer to a
commitment whose symbols each depend on at most $d$ rows ($d=1$ when it opens single neurons). If
the other rows carry no information about the edit's location, it rejects the forgery at most as
often as it could guess the edited neuron in $qd$ tries, even if it is adaptive and reads the whole
trace (Theorem~\ref{thm:guess}). For every such verifier, some ReLU MLP with a hidden layer of width
$N$ admits a single-neuron forgery, with every value in its natural range, that passes with
probability at least $1-qd/N$;
for transformers our evidence is the measured edits above (Remark~\ref{rem:scope}). Conversely,
symbols that mix all rows, such as Reed--Solomon columns, reach error $2^{-\lambda}$ with
$O(\lambda+\log L)$ queries per layer for $L$ layers (Theorem~\ref{thm:dich}). Of the nine
published schemes in Table~\ref{tab:sok}, only two miss a guarantee they state: Anchuri et al.'s full
soundness for binary classifiers, and TensorCommitments'~\cite{tensorcommitments} goal of negligible
soundness, which its weight-only layer selection does not achieve.

\para{A defence that checks everything.} Since no spot-checking verifier beats uniform sampling in
the worst case, ours checks every weight operation. The provider runs the exact int8 model,
bit-identical on its GPU and on the client, and sends every weight operation's result. The verifier
recomputes every weight-free operation, including attention, from checked values, and tests
each weight operation with random linear combinations of its output rows (Freivalds'
algorithm~\cite{freivalds1977}). In mode~$\sC$ it checks each combination against a
Reed--Solomon-encoded, Merkle-committed copy of the weights: the evaluation protocol of a
linear-code polynomial commitment, as in Ligero and Brakedown~\cite{ligero,brakedown}, applied to
every matrix product of the model. A tampered value anywhere is caught except with probability
$2^{-\lambda}$, using only a hash function and a linear code: no SNARK, trusted hardware or trusted
setup. In mode~$\Kpre$ the client instead keeps secret combinations of the weight rows, precomputed
once.

We add four extensions: a one-time setup proof for clients that cannot recompute $C_M$
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-proof}); a Fiat--Shamir transcript that
binds every constant of the computation (Section~\ref{sec:fs}); generated responses proved as one
prefill, each token checked as the greedy choice or that of an exact integer sampler keyed by the
client's seed (Section~\ref{sec:gen}); and V1, which sends int8 values instead of 32-bit results
and reuses the column check (Section~\ref{sec:v1}).

\para{Costs.} On a 2{,}048-token Llama-2-7B or 13B prompt, our prover is \ZkllmSameGpuFactorRange{}$\times$
faster than zkLLM's on the same \PlatformGpu{}, although zkLLM also hides the weights and proves the
weight-free operations\TodoZkllmFactor{}; it is also faster than every other fully sound system
we compare with (Table~\ref{tab:eval-compare}). Proving a 2{,}048-token OPT-1.3B prompt costs
\ServeOptMOverUOneRange{}$\times$ our exact integer forward pass and \ServeOptMOverUTwoRange{}$\times$ an fp16
one. The main cost is the proof, which carries every weight operation's result:
\ProofLlamaLongC{} for a 2{,}048-token Llama-2-7B prompt, whose int8 weights take \WeightsLlama{}.
V1 makes it \VOneRatioRandom{}$\times$ smaller at 2{,}048 tokens but slows the prover. With native exact
kernels, a CPU client in mode~$\Kpre$, holding a \KpreStoreGB{}\,GB key, verifies such a prompt (non-interactive, full model) in \CpuVerifyLong{}\,s, against
\CpuReexecLong{}\,s for exact re-execution with the weights on the same CPU; on a GPU, re-execution is faster\TodoCpuRatioIntro{}. The protocol is not zero-knowledge, so it
targets open-weight models, as do CommitLLM~\cite{commitllm2026} and Gupta et
al.~\cite{gupta2026private}.

\para{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item An attack on spot-checking proofs of inference: a single-site edit followed by honest
  recomputation, which changes OPT-6.7B's output and refutes a published soundness claim
  (Section~\ref{sec:attack}).
  \item A lower bound for spot-checking verifiers, matched up to a factor $O(\lambda+\log L)$
  at every locality by checks that mix rows, and its application to nine published schemes
  (Sections~\ref{sec:sampling}--\ref{sec:sok}).
  \item A fully sound Freivalds-based protocol that needs only a hash function and a linear code,
  and its four extensions (Sections~\ref{sec:defence}--\ref{sec:gen}).
  \item An evaluation on language models up to 13B parameters: a prover
  \ZkllmSameGpuFactorRange{}$\times$ faster than zkLLM on the same GPU, with proof size as the main
  cost (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\para{zkSNARK proofs of inference.} Sum-check and GKR provers~\cite{gkr,zkcnn,zkpytorch,verfcnn},
pairing- and Plonk-based systems~\cite{vcnn,zen,zeno,zkml,ezkl,bionetta} and the VOLE-based
Mystique~\cite{mystique} prove CNNs. zkLLM~\cite{zkllm}, zkGPT~\cite{zkgpt},
DeepProve~\cite{deepprove}, ZKTorch~\cite{zktorch}, Jolt Atlas~\cite{joltatlas} and NanoZK's full
mode~\cite{nanozk} prove language models. They give computational soundness with proofs of under a
kilobyte to tens of megabytes, and many hide the weights, but their provers are slow
(Table~\ref{tab:eval-compare}). DeepProve and zkAgent~\cite{zkagent} prove a generated sequence in
one pass, as we do. V1 borrows the logUp-GKR~\cite{papini2023logupgkr} that such systems use, but
commits no activation.

\para{Spot-checking and sampling.} Table~\ref{tab:sok} describes sampling schemes whose verifiers
fall in the class of
Definition~\ref{def:spot}~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable,slp,commitllm2026,tensorcommitments,nanozk,spex,immaculate,toploc,svip}.
Optimistic schemes~\cite{verde,opml,tao} need an honest party that re-runs every query and
disputes wrong answers. The calculation behind the
edit-then-recompute attack is classical: it underlies proofs of retrievability and data
possession~\cite{juels2007por,ateniese2007pdp}, spot-checkers~\cite{ergun2000spotcheckers} and PCPs
of proximity, which certify only closeness, whereas holographic proofs encode their
input~\cite{bghsv2006robust,bfls1991,chiesa2020fractal}. Theorem~\ref{thm:guess} formalises this
calculation for adaptive, value-aware verifiers that, unlike those of PCPs of proximity,
read the whole trace: honest recomputation moves the change into the weights, so the model, not
the trace, must be encoded. The calculation's tight case is the search for a hidden
object~\cite{chew1967,kadane1971}.

\para{Trusted hardware.} Slalom~\cite{slalom} checks the linear layers of a GPU computation from an
SGX enclave with Freivalds' algorithm. TwinShield~\cite{twinshield} and VeriAttn~\cite{veriattn}
extend enclave-based checking to attention, VeriAttn within a tolerance. GPU confidential computing
runs the whole model inside an attested H100-class GPU, with below 7\% overhead for most LLM
queries~\cite{gpucc}, but trusts the vendor's hardware. We need no trusted hardware, and exact integer semantics leaves no tolerance in which to hide a change.

\para{Freivalds' check and linear-code commitments.} SafetyNets~\cite{safetynets} verifies networks
with quadratic activations by an interactive proof, and ZKML~\cite{zkml} uses Freivalds' check
inside a SNARK. Maverick~\cite{maverick} checks every linear layer of a language model with sparse
Freivalds checks against a client-stored encoded copy of the weights. Gupta et
al.~\cite{gupta2026private} run Freivalds' check in a group's exponent against data that the model
owner generates with secret randomness, for two non-colluding servers.
CommitLLM~\cite{commitllm2026} checks int8 models with secret Freivalds vectors precomputed from the
weights, at 12--14\% serving overhead, and audits attention without verifying it. LAMP~\cite{lamp}
checks a matrix product against a commitment with Freivalds' check and Reed--Solomon proximity
testing inside a Groth16 SNARK. DeepBrake~\cite{deepbrake} merges the testing and evaluation phases
of a row-wise Reed--Solomon commitment into one proximity test for points fixed in advance;
ReedWeave~\cite{reedweave} and BaseFold~\cite{basefold} fold codewords for faster openings;
Section~\ref{sec:setup-proof} bounds what such tests can save on our commitment.

\para{Where we fit.} We keep the client of Anchuri et al., which holds only a weight commitment, and
replace sampling, which needs at least $(1-\varepsilon)N$ paths per layer for soundness error $\varepsilon$, with
a check of every weight operation. Each fully sound protocol above that avoids a SNARK needs more
than a commitment: trusted hardware, a stored encoded copy of the weights, a weight-derived secret
or data generated with secret randomness. To our knowledge, ours is the first Freivalds-based proof
of inference that is fully sound for complete transformers, attention included, and whose client
(in mode~$\sC$) needs none of these; the novelty is this combination, not a new primitive.
