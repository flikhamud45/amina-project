\begin{abstract}
A company that outsources inference to a cloud provider cannot tell whether the provider ran the
agreed model or changed the answer on purpose. Proofs of inference let it check each answer. Those
built on zkSNARKs give strong guarantees but take minutes per query on large models, motivating
lighter protocols that check only a sample of the computation, such as that of Anchuri
et al., where the client re-checks one random path through a committed execution trace. We show
that sampling misses targeted changes: a provider that tampers with a single neuron and computes
everything else honestly almost always passes the path check, on image classifiers and on a real
large language model. No sampling rule,
however it chooses where to look, closes this gap on every network. We therefore build a protocol
that checks every layer with Freivalds' algorithm against a Reed--Solomon/Merkle commitment to the
weights. It needs no SNARK, trusted hardware or trusted setup, a wrong output passes with
negligible probability, and in our experiments it rejected thousands of attacks without a miss. Its
prover is orders of magnitude faster than published zkSNARK provers and faster than every other
system we compare with, and its verifier is faster than most of them and competitive with the
rest; its main cost is a proof that grows with the input.

Consider a company that pays a cloud provider to serve a licensed or fine-tuned language model to
its users. Nothing in a response shows that the provider ran that model with the agreed weights.
The provider may cut costs by serving a cheaper quantised copy, but it may also return a wrong
answer on purpose. A company that handles sensitive data and acts on the model's answers, for
example to approve payments, flag fraud or triage medical records, is exposed to a provider, or an
attacker inside it, that changes the answers to chosen queries and serves every other query
correctly. The company cannot re-run the model to find out, since it lacks the weights
and the hardware; that is why it outsourced inference. A \emph{proof of inference} closes this
gap. With each answer the provider sends a proof, and the client (here, the company) runs a
check, much cheaper than inference, that fails with high probability unless the answer was
computed with the agreed weights on this input.

zkSNARKs~\cite{zkcnn,zkml,zkllm,zkgpt,deepprove} prove that the answer is correct and can keep
the weights secret. Their proofs are usually small and fast to check, but producing one takes far
longer than inference; zkLLM needs
about ten minutes on an A100 for one Llama-2-7B prompt~\cite{zkllm}. The prover's time matters most,
since the provider pays it on every query; the verifier's time comes next, and the proof's size
matters least, provided it stays moderate. Anchuri et al.~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable} proposed a much lighter approach. The provider commits to the
full execution trace with a Merkle tree, and the client checks the computation along \emph{one
random path} from an output neuron back to the input, against a commitment to the weights.
Proving drops from minutes to milliseconds. A different model produces a trace that differs from
the correct one in many places, and a random path is likely to hit one of them. Anchuri et al.\
accordingly prove \emph{other-model} soundness, against a provider that honestly runs a different
model. They note that one path cannot catch a single changed node with probability above $1/N$ in
a layer of width $N$, and they leave \emph{full} soundness, against any wrong answer, open for
LLMs.

We present an attack on this protocol, which we implement and evaluate. A provider that keeps the
committed model and changes only what it needs to can overwrite \emph{a single neuron} and recompute everything after it honestly
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview-attack}). The trace is then inconsistent at
one node, which a random path visits with probability $1/N$, so almost every tampered answer is
accepted. Applied only to inputs that carry a trigger, the edit is a backdoor in the serving
code, which leaves the committed weights clean and answers clean queries bit for bit as before. The
attack carries over to the real OPT-6.7B. Checking many queries does not close the gap, because the
provider chooses which queries to tamper with, and tampering with $k$ of them is caught with
probability at most $k/N$. Nor do the fixes Anchuri et al.\ suggest, sampling more paths or
sampling where it matters: no rule does better than uniform sampling on every network, and
reaching $2^{-40}$ forces the path protocol to open essentially the whole model and trace.

This led us to a different design (Figure~\ref{fig:overview-defence}). The client tests one
random linear combination of all the neurons of every layer (Freivalds'
algorithm~\cite{freivalds1977}). When it does not hold the weights, it checks the combination
against a Reed--Solomon-encoded, Merkle-committed copy of them in the style of
Ligero~\cite{ligero}, so, as in the path protocol, it stores 32 bytes per weight matrix. The model
runs as an int8 network whose results are bit-identical on the provider's GPU and on the client,
which makes the test exact. A tampered value anywhere in the network is then caught with
probability $1-2^{-\lambda}$ instead of $1/N$, and the provider's extra work grows with the model
but not with the input. At $2^{-128}$ our proofs are also smaller than the paths' at $2^{-40}$:
catching the single-neuron attack on a 64-token Llama-2-7B prompt with probability $1-2^{-40}$
takes paths that open about 13.7\,GB, while our proof is 325\,MB.

\para{Contributions.} (1)~\textbf{An attack} (Sections~\ref{sec:attack} and~\ref{sec:break})
that exploits the $1/N$ limit acknowledged in~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable}. One tampered neuron is
accepted with probability $1-1/N$ (99.6--99.8\% on MNIST) and changes the next token of 40 of 40
prompts on the real OPT-6.7B. (2)~\textbf{A bound on sampling-based fixes}
(Section~\ref{sec:sampling}): no sampler beats uniform sampling on every network, and uniform
sampling needs essentially the whole model and trace for $2^{-40}$. (3)~\textbf{A full-soundness
protocol} for whole CNNs and transformers (Sections~\ref{sec:defence}--\ref{sec:opt}), with error
$2^{-\lambda}$, that composes known checks and needs only a hash commitment per weight matrix
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). Optimisations keep the bound and shrink the committed-weights proof 2.0--4.2$\times$ for images
and prompts of up to 512 tokens.
(4)~\textbf{A matched-model evaluation} (Section~\ref{sec:results}) on six image classifiers and
10 LLM architectures up to 13B parameters, against~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable}, 17 published
systems and zkLLM's code on our GPU. Our prover is \pending{35}--32{,}000$\times$ faster than the
zkSNARK provers.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\para{zkSNARK proofs of inference.} A long line of work compiles neural networks into
zero-knowledge proofs. For CNNs these include sum-check/GKR provers~\cite{gkr} such as
zkCNN~\cite{zkcnn} and zkPyTorch~\cite{zkpytorch}, and pairing- or Plonk-based systems such as
vCNN~\cite{vcnn}, ZEN~\cite{zen}, ZENO~\cite{zeno}, ZKML~\cite{zkml}, EZKL~\cite{ezkl} and
Bionetta~\cite{bionetta}; Mystique~\cite{mystique} is an interactive, VOLE-based alternative.
For LLMs they include zkLLM~\cite{zkllm} (also interactive), zkGPT~\cite{zkgpt},
DeepProve~\cite{deepprove}, ZKTorch~\cite{zktorch} and Jolt Atlas~\cite{joltatlas}. They give
computational soundness and mostly small proofs, but their provers are far slower than
inference: from half a second for LeNet-5 to minutes or hours for larger models.

\para{Freivalds-style checks.} SafetyNets~\cite{safetynets} uses interactive proofs for networks
with quadratic activations. Slalom~\cite{slalom} checks every linear layer of a GPU computation
from inside an SGX enclave with Freivalds' algorithm, optionally with a precomputed secret
challenge; TwinShield~\cite{twinshield} and
VeriAttn~\cite{veriattn} extend this to attention inside TEEs. Several recent works need no
enclave. Maverick~\cite{maverick} checks every linear layer of a language model with sparse
Freivalds checks on a code-encoded copy of the weights that the client stores, drawing a fresh
challenge per query. Gupta et al.~\cite{gupta2026private} run Freivalds' check in the exponent of
a group, against verification data that the model owner generates with secret randomness, for a
client that outsources inference to two non-colluding servers. CommitLLM~\cite{commitllm2026}
checks int8 language models with secret Freivalds vectors precomputed from the weights, audits
sampled positions and layers, and leaves attention unverified. LAMP~\cite{lamp} lets a verifier
that holds only a commitment check one matrix product, using Freivalds' check and Reed--Solomon
proximity testing inside a Groth16 SNARK.

\para{Sampling, fingerprints and optimistic protocols.} Anchuri et
al.~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable} spot-check random paths and argue security through trace
separation; SLP~\cite{slp} samples which layers receive a SNARK. TOPLOC~\cite{toploc} and
SVIP~\cite{svip} fingerprint hidden states, with empirical guarantees. Verde~\cite{verde},
opML~\cite{opml} and TAO~\cite{tao} resolve disputes and assume an honest party or an active
challenger.

\para{Where we fit.} We keep the setting of~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable}, a client that holds a
32-byte commitment per weight matrix, but replace its path test, which we break
(Sections~\ref{sec:attack} and~\ref{sec:sampling}), with full soundness $2^{-\lambda}$ for
complete CNNs and transformers (Sections~\ref{sec:defence} and~\ref{sec:protocol}). We chain known
protocols through every layer with an end-to-end soundness proof: Freivalds' check, and the
column check of the Ligero and Brakedown commitments~\cite{ligero,brakedown}. The protocols above
that check every layer without a SNARK each need more than a hash commitment: Slalom an enclave,
Maverick an encoded copy of the weights, CommitLLM a secret key derived from them, and Gupta et
al.\ public data that grows with every matrix and is generated with secret randomness. zkSNARKs
that verify against a Merkle root, such as DeepProve's transparent configuration, need no trusted
setup but prove far more slowly. To our knowledge, ours is the first protocol that is fully sound
for complete CNNs and transformers using only a hash function and a linear code, with no SNARK,
TEE or trusted setup.

\section{Technical Approach}
\label{sec:approach}


\subsection{Setting and threat model}
A model $M$ has a public architecture and secret or public weights. The provider acts as the
prover and the client as the verifier. Before any query the client obtains a commitment $C_M$ to
the weights, for example from the model owner. As in~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable}, we assume
that $C_M$ is computed honestly; unlike a SNARK's trusted setup it involves no secret randomness,
and anyone who holds the weights can recompute it. For each query $x$ the provider returns a
claimed output $y$ and a proof, and may deviate from the protocol arbitrarily. We require
\emph{completeness} (honest answers are always accepted) and \emph{full soundness}: any
$y \neq M(x)$ is rejected except with probability $\varepsilon$ (Anchuri et al.\ prove only
other-model soundness). We
consider three trust settings. In setting $\sC$ the client holds only $C_M$ (32 bytes per weight
matrix), as in~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable} and the zkSNARKs; in $\sK$ it knows the weights; and
$\Kpre$ is $\sK$ with a secret challenge precomputed once per model, as in Slalom's preprocessing
mode and CommitLLM~\cite{commitllm2026}. The architecture includes the integer constants of the operations without
weights (requantisation multipliers, look-up tables), which the client obtains with $C_M$.

\subsection{The path test and the single-neuron attack}
\label{sec:attack}
In the protocol of~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable} (RandPathTest) the prover commits to every
activation $a_j$ of the forward pass with a Merkle tree. The verifier chooses a random output
neuron and walks back to the input, moving to a random parent at each layer. At every node $j$ on
the path it opens the parents' activations and the weights $w_{ij}$ from $C_M$ and checks
$a_j = \phi\big(\sum_{i \in G_j} w_{ij} a_i\big)$, where $G_j$ is the set of parents of $j$ and
$\phi$ the activation. A path opens one weight row per layer, so the proof and the prover's work
stay small.

\para{The single-neuron attack.} Our adversary runs the correct model on $x$, picks one neuron
$j$, and replaces $a_j$ by a value that makes the output wrong (untargeted) or equal to a class of
its choice (targeted). It then recomputes all later layers \emph{with the true weights}. Every
later node is consistent with its parents and every earlier node is untouched, so $j$ is the only
node whose local check fails, the only \emph{inconsistent node}. The verifier notices the attack
only if its path visits $j$, which happens with probability $1/N$ in a layer of width $N$, or
$1-(1-1/N)^s$ with $s$ paths. Applied only to inputs that contain a trigger, the attack becomes a
backdoor in the serving code. The prover keeps the committed weights and answers clean queries
honestly, bit for bit, so no inspection of the weights reveals it and the clean-accuracy gap is
zero by construction.

\subsection{Why sampling does not fix it}
\label{sec:sampling}
Anchuri et al.\ suggest sampling more paths, or sampling where it matters instead of uniformly.
Neither helps against an adversary who knows the rule. Fix a layer of width $N$ and let $U$ be the
set of its neurons whose change can flip the output. If the sampler ignores the claimed values, the
probabilities with which it opens each neuron are fixed before the adversary acts and sum to at
most $s$ over $U$, so the adversary can tamper with a neuron that is opened with probability at
most $s/|U|$, on every network. A sampler that reads the values could do better on a particular
network, but not on every one. We call a sampler \emph{value-aware} if it chooses each neuron to
open from the input, the claimed values, the weights of later layers, each neuron's natural range
(the values it takes on honest inputs) and the rows it has already opened.

\begin{proposition}[no sampler beats uniform on every network]
\label{prop:anysampler}
For every value-aware sampler that opens at most $s$ neurons of a hidden layer of width $N$ and
accepts every network's honest trace, there is a network on which it catches a tampered neuron,
kept within its natural range, with probability at most $s/N$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} Let hidden neuron $i$ copy input bit $\sigma(i)$ for a permutation $\sigma$,
let natural inputs have at most one zero, and let the output be 1 exactly when all hidden neurons
are 1. The adversary sets the neuron that copies the input's zero to 1, which flips the output and
stays in the range $\{0,1\}$. The forged trace, later weights and ranges do not depend on $\sigma$,
and the forged trace is honest for the network in which that neuron copies another bit, so the
sampler rejects only if it opens the tampered row. Every other row it opens shows a neuron that
copies a 1, so for a random $\sigma$ the zero's copier stays uniform among the unopened neurons and
is opened with probability at most $s/N$; some $\sigma$ does no better. \qed

\noindent Opening $s$ distinct neurons uniformly reaches $s/N$ on every dense network, so nothing
beats uniform sampling in the worst case, and catching one tampered neuron with constant
probability needs $s=\Omega(N)$ openings per layer, the cost the protocol was designed to avoid.
The \emph{contribution-weighted sampler}, which steps to parent $i$ with probability proportional
to $|w_{ij} a_i|$, never visits a neuron whose claimed activation is zero, and a floor $\delta$,
sampling by $|w_{ij}|(|a_i|+\delta)$, leaves detection low (Section~\ref{sec:break}).

\subsection{Our defence: checking every layer}
\label{sec:defence}

Since no sampler has a better worst-case guarantee than uniform sampling, our verifier checks every
neuron of every layer.

\para{Exact integers.} The checks compare exact values, but floating-point results differ between
devices and summation orders. The model that is committed and checked is therefore an int8 version
of $M$, obtained by integer-only quantisation~\cite{jacob2018quant}, as many models are served and as
proof systems for inference require~\cite{safetynets,slalom,zkcnn,zen,zkml,zkllm}; Section~\ref{sec:setup}
measures its accuracy. Each weight layer $\ell$
computes $Z_\ell = W_\ell X_\ell + b_\ell$, where $W_\ell$ is int8 with one scale per output
channel, $X_\ell$ is int8 with one scale per tensor, and BatchNorm is folded into $W_\ell$ and
$b_\ell$; convolutions become matrix products through im2col. These integer products can still use
the GPU's fast float32 units: float32 represents every integer below $2^{24}$ exactly, so the
prover sums at most 1{,}024 products of int8 values at a time, as in Slalom~\cite{slalom}, and its
results are bit-identical to the verifier's. Exactness also removes the floating-point tolerance of
the path protocol, which leaves an attacker room to hide small changes.

\para{Three statements.} With the output $y$, the prover sends the pre-activations $Z_\ell$ of every
weight layer, which we call the \emph{claims}. Write $A_\ell = [W_\ell \,|\, b_\ell]$. Between two
weight layers the network applies only operations without weights: requantisation, ReLU, pooling,
residual additions, LayerNorm/RMSNorm, GELU/SiLU (by table look-up), RoPE, softmax, and the
attention products $QK^\T$ and $PV$. The answer $y$ is the last weight layer's pre-activations
(the logits), so it is itself a claim, and it is correct if every claim is. Every claim equals the
value that $M$ computes on $x$ if and only if three statements hold for every weight layer $\ell$:
(S1)~its input $X_\ell$ follows from $x$ and the earlier claims through these operations, for
example $X_\ell = \mathrm{requant}(\mathrm{ReLU}(Z_{\ell-1}))$ in an MLP;
(S2)~its claims are the product of its weights and its input, $Z_\ell = A_\ell[X_\ell; \mathbf 1]$;
and (S3)~$A_\ell$ is the committed matrix.

\para{Checking (S1): recomputation.} The verifier computes every operation without weights itself,
from $x$ and the claims. Each input $X_\ell$ is therefore the verifier's own computation, not a
value sent by the prover, and the checks are chained: a prover cannot pass one layer's check with
one value and feed another into the next. This is also why non-linear layers such as ReLU and
softmax need no proof here: the verifier never checks a claimed activation, it computes it.

\para{Checking (S2): Freivalds' check.} Let $A_\ell$ have $N$ rows (one per output neuron) and row
length $k$, and let $T$ be the number of columns of $X_\ell$ (pixels or tokens); we drop the
subscript $\ell$ from $N$, $k$ and $T$ when the layer is clear. Recomputing $A_\ell[X_\ell;\mathbf
1]$ would cost the verifier as much as inference. Instead it draws $r$ independent random vectors
$\chi \in \F^{N}$ and, for each, checks one random combination of the layer's rows,
\begin{equation}
  \chi^\T Z_\ell \;=\; \underbrace{(\chi^\T A_\ell)}_{u_\ell}\,[X_\ell; \mathbf{1}] ,
  \label{eq:freivalds}
\end{equation}
where we call $u_\ell$ the \emph{folded row}. Let $D = Z_\ell - A_\ell [X_\ell; \mathbf{1}]$ be the
error of the claims: its entry $D_{im}$ is how far the claim of neuron $i$ at position $m$ (a pixel
or token) is from the true value, so a correct layer has $D = 0$. The two sides of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:freivalds} differ by $\chi^\T D$, so the check passes only if $\chi^\T D = 0$. If
some $D_{im} \ne 0$, entry $m$ of $\chi^\T D$ is $\sum_{i'} \chi_{i'} D_{i'm} = \chi_i D_{im} + c$,
where $c$ does not involve $\chi_i$. Whatever the other entries of $\chi$ are, this is zero for
only one of the $p$ values $\chi_i$ can take ($\chi_i = -c/D_{im}$), so a wrong layer passes with
probability at most $1/p$; it must pass the check for each of the $r$ vectors, so at most
$p^{-r}$ in all. This holds however many values are wrong and wherever they are: a
single tampered neuron is caught as reliably as a wholly wrong layer. Given $u_\ell$ (cost
$O(rNk)$), the two sides cost $O(r(N+k)T)$, against $O(NkT)$ for recomputing the layer.

We work in the BabyBear field $\F$, $p = 15\cdot2^{27}+1 \approx 2^{31}$. Integers map into $\F$
without wrap-around as long as they are small, and two checks ensure this. The verifier
range-checks every claim ($|z| < 2^{29}$), and setup checks that every honest pre-activation is
in the same range: $128\sum_c |w_{ic}| + |b_i| < 2^{29}$ for every row $i$ of a product, whose
inputs are int8 and so at most 128 in magnitude (for an embedding, $\max_c |w_{ic}| + |b_i|$). A wrong claim and the correct value then differ by less than
$2^{30} < p$, so they remain distinct in $\F$.

\para{Checking (S3): committed weights.} In $\sK$ and $\Kpre$ the verifier holds the weights and
computes $u_\ell$ itself, so (S3) holds by construction. In setting $\sC$ it cannot, so the prover
sends $u_\ell$, and the verifier must check that $u_\ell = \chi^\T A_\ell$ for the committed
$A_\ell$. Opening a few entries of $u_\ell$ would miss one changed entry, as a path misses one
neuron, so we commit to an \emph{encoded} copy of the weights, as in the Ligero and Brakedown commitments~\cite{ligero,brakedown}.
At setup every row of $A_\ell$ is Reed--Solomon encoded~\cite{reedsolomon1960}: the polynomial
whose coefficients are the row is evaluated at $n_\ell = 4\bar k$ points, where $\bar k \ge k$ is a
power of two (Section~\ref{sec:opt} relaxes this), giving $\hat A_\ell$. The commitment $C_M$ holds one Merkle root~\cite{merkle1987} per weight
matrix, over the columns of $\hat A_\ell$. Encoding is linear, so $\chi^\T \hat A_\ell =
\enc(\chi^\T A_\ell)$. The verifier encodes the claimed $u_\ell$ itself, opens $t$ random columns
$c$ with their Merkle paths, and checks $\chi^\T \hat A_{\ell,c} = \enc(u_\ell)_c$. If $u_\ell$ is
wrong, $\enc(u_\ell)$ and $\enc(\chi^\T A_\ell)$ are distinct codewords whose difference is a
nonzero polynomial of degree below $k$, so they agree on at most $k-1$ of the $n_\ell$ positions,
and $t$ distinct columns all miss the difference with probability at most
$((k-1)/n_\ell)^t < 4^{-t}$. Because $C_M$ is computed honestly, every committed row is a codeword
and no proximity test is needed.

\subsection{Protocol and soundness}
\label{sec:protocol}
Setup happens once per model: the owner encodes and commits the weights and publishes $C_M$.
Figure~\ref{fig:protocol} shows one query of the basic protocol, with $r$ vectors $\chi$ and $t$
columns per layer;
besides recomputing the operations without weights (S1) and the checks of (S2) and (S3), the
verifier range-checks the claims.

\begin{theorem}[soundness]
\label{thm:sound}
If $C_M$ is honest and the prover cannot find a hash collision, then for any claimed
$y \neq M(x)$ the verifier accepts with probability at most
$\varepsilon = \sum_\ell \big[\,p^{-r} + \big(\tfrac{k_\ell - 1}{n_\ell}\big)^{t}\,\big]$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\emph{Proof.} Some claim is wrong, since $y$ is one; let $\ell$ be the first layer with a
wrong claim. Its input $X_\ell$ is correct, because the verifier computed it from $x$ and correct
earlier claims (S1), and the range check keeps the wrong claim distinct from the true value in
$\F$. So $Z_\ell \neq A_\ell[X_\ell;\mathbf 1]$ for the committed $A_\ell$, and the prover passes
only by fooling a random check: with the true $u_\ell$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:freivalds} fails except
with probability $p^{-r}$ (S2); with another $u_\ell$, a column check fails except with
probability $((k_\ell-1)/n_\ell)^t$ (S3), since the opened columns are the committed ones unless
the prover finds a hash collision. A union bound over the layers finishes the proof. \qed

\para{Parameters.} For a target $\lambda$ and $L$ weight layers we set
$\beta = \lambda + \log_2(2L)$, $r = \lceil \beta/\log_2 p \rceil$ and $t = \lceil \beta/2
\rceil$; since $n_\ell \ge 4k_\ell$, each of the $2L$ terms of $\varepsilon$ is at most
$2^{-\beta}$, so $\varepsilon \le 2^{-\lambda}$ (for VGG-16 at $\lambda{=}128$, $r{=}5$ and
$t{=}67$). Interactively, each challenge is expanded with SHAKE-256 from a fresh 256-bit random
key; deriving the keys from a hash of the transcript (Fiat--Shamir~\cite{fiatshamir1986})
makes the protocol non-interactive. It is round-by-round sound~\cite{canetti2019fs} (once the
claims are fixed only the first wrong layer's $\chi$ can make them pass, and once $u_\ell$ is fixed
only that layer's columns can), so a prover that makes $Q$ random-oracle queries succeeds with
probability at most $Q\,2^{-\beta}$ plus that of finding a collision~\cite{holmgren2019rbr}, which
64 extra bits in $\beta$ keep below $2^{-\lambda}$ for $Q \le 2^{64}$.

\para{Known weights.} In $\sK$ the verifier computes $u_\ell$ itself and messages (3)--(6) are
omitted. In $\Kpre$ it computes the secret $\chi$ and $u_\ell$ once per model, so the proof is
the claims alone. The prover learns each verdict and could repeat an accepted forgery, but until
one is accepted the verdicts do not depend on $\chi$, so a false acceptance occurs among $Q$
queries that reuse $\chi$ with probability at most $Q\sum_\ell p^{-r}$.

\subsection{Costs and optimisations}
\label{sec:opt}

\subsubsection{\textbf{Costs.}} The prover runs the forward pass, computes $u_\ell$ and opens $t$ columns, which it
recomputes from $W_\ell$; its extra work, $O((r+t)\sum_\ell N_\ell k_\ell)$, does not depend on the
number of pixels or tokens. With 4-byte claims and field elements and 32-byte hashes, a proof in
setting $\sC$ has
\begin{equation}
  |\pi| = 4\sum_\ell N_\ell T_\ell + 4r\sum_\ell k_\ell + 4t\sum_\ell N_\ell + 32\sum_\ell h_\ell
  \label{eq:size}
\end{equation}
bytes: the claims, which grow with the input, and the folded rows, the $t$ opened columns of $N_\ell$
entries and the $h_\ell \le t\log_2 n_\ell$ hashes of each layer's Merkle multiproof, which do not.
For Llama-2-7B at 64 tokens these are 349, 23, 385 and 4\,MB of the basic protocol's 762\,MB
proof. In $\sK$ and
$\Kpre$ the proof is the claims alone.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Optimisations.}} The parameters above keep the soundness argument short but leave the proof
larger than it needs to be. We call the protocol so far the \emph{basic} protocol, and the one with
the four changes below and the engineered verifier described after them the \emph{optimised}
protocol, which Section~\ref{sec:results} measures and Section~\ref{sec:effect} compares with the
basic one. The changes alter only how the weights are committed and checked, which positions of
the last block are proved for a next-token output, and how messages are sent. Theorem~\ref{thm:sound}
still holds, with one term per encoded matrix $j$ (a looked-up embedding table adds none): $p^{-r}$,
plus the chance that its $t_j$ distinct opened columns all fall among the at most $m_j-1$ of its
$n_j$ positions where two distinct codewords of message length $m_j$ agree.

\para{Fewer columns.} The basic protocol opens $t$ columns of every matrix, each under its own
Merkle tree. A column exposes a wrong value unless it lands where the honest and the claimed codeword agree, so a
codeword long relative to its message needs fewer columns. The optimised protocol opens
in each tree just enough columns to keep every term below $2^{-\beta}$, and matrices with codewords
of equal length can share one tree and multiproof. A public \emph{planning rule} picks, of six
candidate plans of codeword lengths and layouts, the one sending the fewest bytes per query
besides the claims, with at most twice the basic protocol's encoded entries at setup (or $2^{34}$
if more); the plan follows from public shapes and is part of $C_M$. LeNet-5's matrices then share
one tree of length $2^{18}$ and open 15 columns instead of 66 in each of five
trees.

\para{Transposed layout.} A layer's opening costs $rk+tN$ field elements, too many for a wide layer
such as GPT-2's output layer ($N{=}50{,}257$). For such layers the owner instead commits the
encoded rows $\hat A'$ of $A^\T$, and the verifier combines positions rather than neurons: it draws
$r$ vectors $\chi' \in \F^{T}$ (the $T$ unit vectors when $T \le r$), computes $z = Z\chi'$ and
$w = [X;\mathbf 1]\chi'$ itself for each, and checks $\enc(z)_c = (w^\T \hat A')_c$ on $t$ opened
columns of $k$ entries. Since $w^\T\hat A' = \enc(Aw)$, a wrong claim passes only if all $r$
products $(Z-A[X;\mathbf 1])\chi'$ vanish or all $t$ columns fall where two distinct codewords
agree (here $m_j$ counts the stacked rows). No folded row is sent, and the opening
shrinks to $tk$ field elements; projections of one input may share one such matrix.

\para{Decoders.} An embedding table without a bias is only looked up, so, as in
CommitLLM~\cite{commitllm2026}, it is committed as a Merkle tree over its rows and the prover opens
the rows it used; a wrong row would need a hash collision. A next-token output depends only on the
last position, so the last block is computed there alone, except for the keys and values that
attention needs at every position.

\para{Compact encoding.} An int8 model's claims need far fewer than the 32 bits we count for each.
Each layer's claims are bit-packed at a width chosen for it, the few
values that do not fit sent separately (frame-of-reference and Rice coding), and field elements
in 31 bits; a claim then takes 16--19 bits. The verifier decodes before checking; Fiat--Shamir
absorbs the encoded bytes. In $\sK$ and $\Kpre$, which open no columns, only this change and the
previous one apply, the verifier reading the embedding rows itself.

\para{Verifier engineering.} Without changing a proof byte, the optimised verifier evaluates
$\enc(u_\ell)$ only at the opened positions, batches its exact products, computes attention in
int32 (as the released prover also does) and streams the claims to a GPU.

\subsection{Breaking the path protocol}
\label{sec:break}

\para{Reproduction.} Our implementation behaves as Anchuri et al.\ describe. On MNIST versions of
four of their substitute-model settings, honest provers were accepted in all 3{,}000 runs of each,
and substitute models were caught in 99.8--100\% of runs, even an 8-bit quantised copy that agrees
with $M$ on all 150 evaluated queries. Without the prescribed tolerance of $10^{-4}$, an honest prover is accepted in
21--37\% of runs, because a layer-wide matrix product and a single-neuron dot product round
differently.

\para{Attacks on MNIST.} We compute each attack's exact probability of passing one path by dynamic
programming over the paths. The forgeries of~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable} (its substitute model,
Appendices~E and~F and Section~7.2) corrupt hundreds of the MLP's 778 trace nodes and are always
caught, while ours leave 1--14 inconsistent nodes. One tampered neuron is accepted with the predicted probability $1-1/N$
(99.6--99.8\%). Triggered by a
$3{\times}3$ bright patch, the backdoor returns the honest output on every clean query and
succeeds on every triggered query not already in the target class. It passed 99.96\% of 2{,}300
end-to-end runs, within sampling error of the predicted 99.8\%. Keeping each tampered value below
its neuron's 95th percentile takes about five neurons and still passes 99\% of the time under
uniform sampling. With 250
paths, the single-neuron attack is accepted 61\% of the time.

\para{Other samplers.} Against an adversary who knows the rule, uniform sampling detects 0.20\%
of attacks, a static importance score (outgoing weight magnitude) 0.11\% and gradient saliency
0.016\%. The contribution-weighted sampler detects nothing: zeroing about 14 of the 512 neurons,
their most common value (65\% of inputs), flips the prediction, and end-to-end runs accepted
2{,}000 of 2{,}000 attacks. A floor
$\delta=1$ raises detection to 2\% (10$\times$ uniform); other floors give 0.9--6$\times$.

\para{Larger models.} On every CNN and every test query, changing one penultimate neuron suffices
to flip the prediction. One path catches it with probability 1/84 (LeNet-5) to 1/512 (VGG,
ResNet), and a change to the least-visited neuron with probability as low as 1/28 million. This
includes the dog-vs-cat ResNet-18, a binary classifier, for which Anchuri et al.\ argue that their
analysis extends to full soundness because the adversary can only flip the output. Reaching $2^{-40}$ takes 2{,}316--14{,}182 paths, which open the whole model and trace
(Figure~\ref{fig:security}). On Llama-2-7B one path detects the attack with probability
1/11{,}008, and $2^{-40}$ needs about 305{,}000 paths ($\approx$13.7\,GB with the fp16 weights
of~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable}, at 64 tokens).

\para{A real LLM.} On the real OPT-6.7B (fp16), the attacker ranks a few FFN neurons of layers 8,
16, 24 and 31 at the last position, by gradient and by their direct effect on the logits. It then
finds the smallest change to one of them that changes the next token. One neuron sufficed for
all 40 prompts. A fixed-target backdoor is more constrained: because of the final LayerNorm, a
last-layer neuron can force only its \emph{saturation token}, so the 16{,}384 neurons reach
6{,}187 of the 50{,}272 tokens. The backdoor succeeds for ``\texttt{back}'' (40/40 triggered
prompts, clean prompts unchanged) and fails for ``\texttt{hacked}'' (0/40). Its values exceed the
neurons' natural range, which the path protocol does not check; one path detects it with
probability 1/16{,}384, and $2^{-40}$ needs 454{,}248 paths.

\subsection{Soundness in practice}
\label{sec:security}
\para{Outcomes.} In the optimised runs the verifier accepted all \OptNHonest{} honest queries and rejected all
\OptNAttacks{} attacks. The \OptNAttacksCNN{} image-model attacks, at $\lambda{=}40$ with
committed weights, were one changed value, one changed penultimate neuron, one changed logit, 1\%
of the weights perturbed, the substitute $\tilde M$, and two forgeries that each
pass every check but one. Every language-model run added $+1$ to one middle-layer pre-activation
(\OptNAttacksPlusOne{} attacks, \OptNAttacksPlusOnePartial{} of them on 1- and
2-block builds of the 30--70B shapes), and for five models from OPT-1.3B to
Llama-2-13B we also ran the image-model attacks that apply to them. The basic protocol's
run accepted all \NHonest{} honest queries and rejected all \NAttacks{} attacks (\NAttacksLLM{}~on
language models).

\para{Each check is needed.} Freivalds' check rejected
\OptNFreivalds{} attacks and the column check against the code \OptNColumnsCode{}, including every
forged folded row and every tampered claim of a layer in the transposed layout; the Merkle check
rejected all \OptNColumnsMerkle{} forged columns (a range check caught the remaining two).

\para{Summary.} Our prover is the fastest of every system we compare with, by orders of magnitude
against the zkSNARKs: it proves a query in milliseconds for the image models and in seconds for
the language models. Our verifier is faster in most rows and slower only than ZKML's and zkLLM's, whose provers are so much slower than ours that proving each
query with them is barely practical.
Against zkCNN on LeNet-5, DeepProve on GPT-2 and Maverick we are better on all three costs.
We get there with only hashing and Freivalds' check, without a SNARK or a trusted setup; the one
cost we pay is a larger proof, which grows with the input.

\section{Discussion}

\para{Implications.} Anchuri et al.'s path protocol still catches a provider that substitutes a
cheaper model, but one that wants to change a particular answer passes it almost surely, so it
should not be relied on where a wrong answer is costly. Our protocol catches such a provider except
with probability $2^{-\lambda}$, at a prover cost of milliseconds to seconds per query and without
a zkSNARK or a trusted setup, which makes checking every answer practical. MLPs and CNNs are
verified in milliseconds on an ordinary CPU; for language models the verifier needs a GPU and a
fast link, so it fits a gateway or an auditor rather than an end device.

\para{Limitations.} (i)~The proof grows with the activations; at 2{,}048 tokens it is
mostly claims and gigabytes for 7B models, against zkLLM's 157--183\,kB. (ii)~The verifier's cost
grows with the square of the prompt length: at 2{,}048 tokens the CPU verifier needs up to
\pending{3.7}\,min, and \pendingclaim{the GPU verifier is \pending{2.9--3.1}$\times$ slower than
zkLLM's}. (iii)~The provider must serve the integer model,

% Sec. 5.5 (withheld: excluded_cells.csv; placeholders: basic). Measured: 1.1x faster than zkLLM's
% on OPT-125M, 2.9x slower on OPT-1.3B
as proof systems for inference require~\cite{safetynets,slalom,zkcnn,zen,zkml,zkllm}, at some cost
in accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). (iv)~Like the path protocol we assume an honest weight commitment, which the
planning rule's longer codewords make up to 13$\times$ slower to compute; a malformed one would need a proximity
argument as in Ligero. (v)~The opened columns reveal random linear combinations of each weight
row, so the protocol is not zero-knowledge. 
% (vi)~We prove one forward pass, without a KV cache. (vii)~The prototype is Python, and the same-GPU zkLLM comparison rests on its demo.

\para{Future work.} The proof is mostly claims, which the verifier needs in full to recompute the
operations without weights; proving those operations with a sum-check argument, as zkLLM does,
would let it check them at a few random points instead, at the cost of more prover work. Checking the attention products instead of
recomputing them, with Freivalds' check on claimed scores or a sum-check argument as in
zkLLM~\cite{zkllm}, could make the verifier much faster at long prompts, at some cost in proof
size. A native verifier and a KV cache would also
help.

\section{Conclusion}
Sampling a computation is not enough to verify it. Against the path protocol of Anchuri
et al.~\cite{anchuri2026verifiable}, a provider that tampers with a single neuron and computes
everything else honestly almost always passes, on image classifiers and on the real OPT-6.7B, and
no sampling rule, however it chooses where to look, closes this gap on every network. A provider
that wants to change a particular answer can therefore do so unnoticed.

We close the gap by checking every layer instead of a sample: Freivalds' algorithm against a
Reed--Solomon/Merkle commitment to the weights accepts a wrong output with probability at most
$2^{-\lambda}$, and in our experiments the protocol rejected all \NAttacks{} attacks on the basic
protocol and all \OptNAttacks{} on the optimised one. It needs only hashing, with no SNARK, trusted
hardware or trusted setup, and it is practical: its prover is the fastest of all the systems we
compared, orders of magnitude faster than the zkSNARK provers; its verifier is faster in most
comparisons; and against zkCNN on LeNet-5, DeepProve on GPT-2 and Maverick it is better on all
three costs. Its remaining cost is a proof that grows with the input, which makes long prompts its
weak point and the natural target for future work.
